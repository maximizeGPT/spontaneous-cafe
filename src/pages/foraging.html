What the day is

A walk with baskets, and a route Matt picks that morning.

He reads the tide chart the night before and checks what has come up since the last rain, then sets the route. You carry a basket. He carries the knife and the bags, and thirty years of knowing which ridge holds chanterelles in a dry November. Nothing goes in a basket until he has identified it.

Couples drive up from San Francisco for a weekend and give one day of it to this. Families book it for kids who want to hold something they picked. Small groups split one price four or six ways, which is how most friends do it. One couple spent a day of their engagement trip on the coast, harvested salt at low tide, filled two baskets with mushrooms and ate all of it that night in their rental.

Nobody needs to arrive knowing anything. Most people can name one wild plant walking in and fifteen walking out.

Matt, on why the route is never the same twice: “Thirty years in, and I still learn a road I had not walked.”

The full day

Four stops, in this order, because the tide goes first.

Times move with the tide chart. The shape of the day holds. A three-hour excursion takes one of these stops and a six-hour takes two or three.

  1. Low tide

    Salt off the rocks

    Seawater and sea vegetables out of the splash zone. Five gallons boils down at home to about a cup of salt. It goes in a jar and comes with you.

  2. Late morning

    Into the forest

    Mushrooms, greens and berries under the redwoods. Matt puts the edible one and the thing that looks exactly like it side by side, before either goes near a basket.

  3. Midday

    Farm stands and the market

    Unstaffed stands on back roads where you leave cash in a box. Yours is usually the only car there. Whatever the stands are short on comes from the farmers market.

  4. Evening

    Dinner from the baskets

    Matt cooks a multi-course meal in your kitchen out of what came home. You sit down. The twelve-hour day ends this way.

What we find

Four seasons on this coast, and four different baskets.

Rain, fog and tide move all of this by weeks. The list below is what a good day looks like in each season.

Nettles want gloves. Miner's lettuce covers whole banks in April and eats like a mild spinach.

Summer baskets

Shore

Line-caught out of Noyo Harbor. Salmon depends on whether the season is open that summer.

September to November

Autumn baskets

Forest

Chanterelles

Two weeks after the first real rain, on the same ridges every year. It is the busiest stretch of Matt's year.

Forest

Boletes, hedgehogs

Boletes get eaten by bugs from the inside, so Matt shows you how to check the stem before you cut.

Forest

Black trumpets, late

They are the color of wet leaf litter. You walk over them for ten minutes before your eyes adjust.

Roadside

Huckleberries

Small and dark. An hour of picking fills one quart, which is enough for a galette.

Farm

Apples from Anderson Valley and Philo

Varieties that never reach a supermarket, sold out of crates by the road.

Farm

Winter squash, quince

Quince is inedible raw and turns pink when you cook it slowly.

Sample of an autumn day. Mushroom timing follows the rain, so autumn dates move by a week or two.

December to February

Winter baskets

Forest

Candy caps

They smell like maple syrup once dried, and one small bag flavors desserts for a year.

Forest

Yellowfoot, black trumpets

Both keep coming through cold rain, which is what puts Matt in the woods in January.

Shore

Dungeness crab, sea urchin

Crab is at its best in winter. Urchin comes out of the shell and onto bread within the hour.

Shore

Beach salt

Any low tide. Winter water is cleaner, and the wind does half the drying.

Farm

Kale, chard

Cold makes both sweeter. The stands have them when they have almost nothing else.

Farm

Citrus from inland

Lemons and mandarins come from warmer valleys an hour away.

Also out there

Three other ways to spend a day outside.

Same three lengths and the same rates. Any of them can end with dinner.

Forest bathing and nature excursions

Slow walking and frequent stops, with very little talking. Matt names what is edible when you ask, though the day is built around being under the trees, and most baskets come home empty. People who book this usually arrive tired from a city.

Farm and ranch tours

Matt has bought from these farms for years, so the route goes where he already knows the growers. You see the ground the lamb walked on and the row the greens came out of. It changes how the same dinner tastes that night.

Interactive dinner parties

Your group cooks the meal with Matt instead of watching it arrive. Some people want a knife in their hand and some want a glass of wine and a stool, and both work at the same party. Everyone eats at the same table at the end.

Before you book

The five things people ask Matt.

What should I wear and bring?

Layers, and boots or shoes you are happy to get wet. The coast runs cold and foggy in summer and wet in winter, so bring a rain shell in any season. Water and a snack help on the long day. Leave the white jacket at the rental.

What happens if the weather turns?

We go out in rain and fog, and some of the best mushroom days are the worst weather days. If a storm makes the roads or the rocks unsafe I move the route inland or we move the date. Cancellation terms are worth confirming with me when you book.

Can we bring kids?

Yes, and families book this often. Children are better than adults at spotting things low to the ground. For younger kids I usually suggest three or six hours, and I keep the walking short and the beach in the plan.

Is it safe to eat what we find?

I have foraged for over thirty years, in Northern California and well outside it. I teach the lookalikes first, so you learn the dangerous one before the good one. Anything either of us is unsure about stays in the woods, and I check every basket again before I cook out of it.

How far do we drive?

We stay in the greater Mendocino area, and most days total under an hour of driving spread across several stops. Some of it is on back roads and gravel. Tell me where you are staying and I will build the route around it.
